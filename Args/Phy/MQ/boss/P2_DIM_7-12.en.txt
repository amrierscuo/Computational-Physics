# DIM 7 - Time-independent perturbation theory: non-degenerate and degenerate cases
**(development in \(\lambda\), corrections \(E^{(1)}\), \(|\psi^{(1)}\rangle\), \(E^{(2)}\); degenerate case: diagonalization of \(H'\) in subspace and secular equation)**

- **Core (degenerate):** P2·25B `img/224.jpg`-`img/227.jpg`

- **Support (bra-ket / completeness):** P1·12 `img/126.jpg`-`img/132.jpg`

# 0) Goal "boss" (what you need to do without hesitation)
1) Set:
\[
H = H_0 + \lambda H', \qquad \lambda\ll 1,
\]
and put the equation to stationary eigenvalues:
\[
H|\Psi_n\rangle = E_n|\Psi_n\rangle.
\]

2) **Non-degenerate case**: obtain (until order II)
\[
\boxed{E_n^{(1)}=\langle \phi_n|H'|\phi_n\rangle}
\]
\[
\boxed{|\Psi_n^{(1)}\rangle=\sum_{m\neq n}\frac{\langle \phi_m|H'|\phi_n\rangle}{E_n^{(0)}-E_m^{(0)}}\,|\phi_m\rangle}
\]
\[
\boxed{E_n^{(2)}=\sum_{m\neq n}\frac{|\langle \phi_m|H'|\phi_n\rangle|^2}{E_n^{(0)}-E_m^{(0)}}}
\]
and say **validity condition**:
\[
|\lambda\,H'_{mn}|\ll |E_n^{(0)}-E_m^{(0)}|.
\]

3) **Degenerate Case**: Say the correct procedure well:
- If \(E_n^{(0)}\) is \(r_n\)-degenerate, first **diagonalize \(H'\)** in degenerate subspace \(S_n\);
- Solve the **secular equation**:
\[
\boxed{\det\!\big(H'_{ij}-E^{(1)}\delta_{ij}\big)=0},
\qquad
H'_{ij}=\langle \phi_n^{(i)}|H'|\phi_n^{(j)}\rangle,
\]
getting \(r_n\) corrections \(E_{n,a}^{(1)}\) and combinations “good” \(|\phi_{n,a}^{(0)}\rangle\).

4) Know how to write also (after choosing the "good" eigenstates in the subspace):
\[
\boxed{|\Psi_{n,a}^{(1)}\rangle=\sum_{m\notin S_n}\frac{\langle \phi_m|H'|\phi_{n,a}^{(0)}\rangle}{E_n^{(0)}-E_m^{(0)}}\,|\phi_m\rangle},
\qquad
\boxed{E_{n,a}^{(2)}=\sum_{m\notin S_n}\frac{|\langle \phi_m|H'|\phi_{n,a}^{(0)}\rangle|^2}{E_n^{(0)}-E_m^{(0)}}}.
\]

5) Example: "Safe shot" \(r_n=2\) (splitting):
\[
\det\begin{pmatrix}
H'_{11}-E^{(1)} & H'_{12}\\
H'_{21} & H'_{22}-E^{(1)}
\end{pmatrix}=0
\Rightarrow
\boxed{E_\pm^{(1)}=\frac{H'_{11}+H'_{22}}{2}\pm\frac12\sqrt{(H'_{11}-H'_{22})^2+4|H'_{12}|^2}}.
\]

---

# 1) General setup (perturbative expansion)
Assume the eigenvalues and eigenstates are known of \(H_0\):
\[
H_0|\phi_n\rangle = E_n^{(0)}|\phi_n\rangle,
\qquad
\langle \phi_m|\phi_n\rangle=\delta_{mn}.
\]

I will introduce a *stationary* \(H'\) perturbation and a count parameter (s) of \(\lambda\):
\[
H = H_0+\lambda H',\qquad \lambda\ll 1.
\]

I'm looking for a solution of:
\[
H|\Psi_n\rangle = E_n|\Psi_n\rangle,
\]
expanding in series:
\[
E_n = E_n^{(0)}+\lambda E_n^{(1)}+\lambda^2E_n^{(2)}+\dots
\]
\[
|\Psi_n\rangle = |\phi_n\rangle+\lambda|\Psi_n^{(1)}\rangle+\lambda^2|\Psi_n^{(2)}\rangle+\dots
\]

**Intermediate normalization (clipboard):**
\[
\boxed{\langle \phi_n|\Psi_n\rangle=1}
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
\langle \phi_n|\Psi_n^{(1)}\rangle=0,\ \langle \phi_n|\Psi_n^{(2)}\rangle=0,\dots
\]
(it is used to make corrections unique and to make certain terms disappear).

---

# 2) Non-degenerate case - demonstration of \(E_n^{(1)}\), \(|\Psi_n^{(1)}\rangle\), \(E_n^{(2)}\)

## 2A) Order \(\lambda^0\)
Replace expansions to \(H|\Psi_n\rangle=E_n|\Psi_n\rangle\) and take the end to \(\lambda^0\):
\[
H_0|\phi_n\rangle = E_n^{(0)}|\phi_n\rangle
\]
(true by definition).

## 2B) Order \(\lambda^1\)
The term at \(\lambda^1\) gives:
\[
H_0|\Psi_n^{(1)}\rangle + H'|\phi_n\rangle
=
E_n^{(0)}|\Psi_n^{(1)}\rangle + E_n^{(1)}|\phi_n\rangle.
\]
I'll take it all to the left:
\[
\boxed{(H_0-E_n^{(0)})|\Psi_n^{(1)}\rangle + (H'-E_n^{(1)})|\phi_n\rangle=0.}
\]

### (i) Derivation of \(E_n^{(1)}\)
Multiply left by \(\langle \phi_n|\). The first term disappears because
\(\langle\phi_n|(H_0-E_n^{(0)})=0\).
I get:
\[
\langle \phi_n|H'|\phi_n\rangle - E_n^{(1)}\langle \phi_n|\phi_n\rangle=0
\Rightarrow
\boxed{E_n^{(1)}=\langle \phi_n|H'|\phi_n\rangle.}
\]

### (ii) Derivation of \(|\Psi_n^{(1)}\rangle\)
Expand \(|\Psi_n^{(1)}\rangle\) based on \(H_0\):
\[
|\Psi_n^{(1)}\rangle=\sum_{m}c_{nm}^{(1)}|\phi_m\rangle.
\]
The intermediate Normalization requires \(c_{nn}^{(1)}=\langle\phi_n|\Psi_n^{(1)}\rangle=0\), therefore:
\[
|\Psi_n^{(1)}\rangle=\sum_{m\neq n}c_{nm}^{(1)}|\phi_m\rangle.
\]

Multiply the order equation 1 by \(\langle \phi_m|\) with \(m\neq n\):
\[
\langle \phi_m|(H_0-E_n^{(0)})|\Psi_n^{(1)}\rangle + \langle \phi_m|H'|\phi_n\rangle = 0
\]
because \(\langle \phi_m|\phi_n\rangle=0\) when \(m\neq n\).

Time:
\[
\langle \phi_m|H_0|\Psi_n^{(1)}\rangle
=\sum_{k\neq n}c_{nk}^{(1)}\langle \phi_m|H_0|\phi_k\rangle
=\sum_{k\neq n}c_{nk}^{(1)}E_k^{(0)}\delta_{mk}
= c_{nm}^{(1)}E_m^{(0)}.
\]
So:
\[
c_{nm}^{(1)}(E_m^{(0)}-E_n^{(0)}) + \langle \phi_m|H'|\phi_n\rangle=0
\]
\[
\Rightarrow
\boxed{c_{nm}^{(1)}=\frac{\langle \phi_m|H'|\phi_n\rangle}{E_n^{(0)}-E_m^{(0)}}.}
\]

I'm replacing:
\[
\boxed{
|\Psi_n^{(1)}\rangle=\sum_{m\neq n}\frac{\langle \phi_m|H'|\phi_n\rangle}{E_n^{(0)}-E_m^{(0)}}\,|\phi_m\rangle.}
\]

## 2C) Order \(\lambda^2\) and \(E_n^{(2)}\)
The term at \(\lambda^2\) gives:
\[
H_0|\Psi_n^{(2)}\rangle + H'|\Psi_n^{(1)}\rangle
=
E_n^{(0)}|\Psi_n^{(2)}\rangle + E_n^{(1)}|\Psi_n^{(1)}\rangle + E_n^{(2)}|\phi_n\rangle.
\]
Port to the left:
\[
(H_0-E_n^{(0)})|\Psi_n^{(2)}\rangle + (H'-E_n^{(1)})|\Psi_n^{(1)}\rangle - E_n^{(2)}|\phi_n\rangle=0.
\]

I multiply left by \(\langle \phi_n|\). The first term disappears and, using intermediate normalization (\(\langle \phi_n|\Psi_n^{(1)}\rangle=0\)), I get:
\[
\langle \phi_n|H'|\Psi_n^{(1)}\rangle - E_n^{(2)}=0
\Rightarrow
\boxed{E_n^{(2)}=\langle \phi_n|H'|\Psi_n^{(1)}\rangle.}
\]

Replace \(|\Psi_n^{(1)}\rangle=\sum_{m\neq n}c_{nm}^{(1)}|\phi_m\rangle\):
\[
E_n^{(2)}=\sum_{m\neq n}c_{nm}^{(1)}\langle \phi_n|H'|\phi_m\rangle
=\sum_{m\neq n}\frac{\langle \phi_m|H'|\phi_n\rangle\langle \phi_n|H'|\phi_m\rangle}{E_n^{(0)}-E_m^{(0)}}.
\]
If \(H'\) is hermitian, \(\langle \phi_n|H'|\phi_m\rangle = \langle \phi_m|H'|\phi_n\rangle^*\), then:
\[
\boxed{E_n^{(2)}=\sum_{m\neq n}\frac{|\langle \phi_m|H'|\phi_n\rangle|^2}{E_n^{(0)}-E_m^{(0)}}.}
\]

**Energy summary to order II (later \(\lambda=1\)):**
\[
\boxed{
E_n \simeq E_n^{(0)}+\lambda\langle \phi_n|H'|\phi_n\rangle
+\lambda^2\sum_{m\neq n}\frac{|\langle \phi_m|H'|\phi_n\rangle|^2}{E_n^{(0)}-E_m^{(0)}}.}
\]

---

# 3) Why the expansion fails near degeneracy (validity condition)
In the mixing coefficient there is:
\[
c_{nm}^{(1)}=\frac{H'_{mn}}{E_n^{(0)}-E_m^{(0)}}.
\]
If \(E_n^{(0)}\approx E_m^{(0)}\) the denominator is small ⇒ the correction explodes.

So the practical condition is:
\[
\boxed{|\lambda H'_{mn}|\ll |E_n^{(0)}-E_m^{(0)}| \quad \forall\,m\neq n.}
\]

If there is real degeneracy (\(E_n^{(0)}=E_m^{(0)}\)), the non-degenerate method should be replaced by the **degenerate method**.

---

# 4) Degenerate case - secular equation and good states
Suppose a \(E_n^{(0)}\) level is \(r_n\)-fold degenerate. There is an orthonormal base of degenerate subspace \(S_n\):
\[
H_0|\phi_n^{(i)}\rangle = E_n^{(0)}|\phi_n^{(i)}\rangle,
\qquad
i=1,\dots,r_n,
\qquad
\langle \phi_n^{(i)}|\phi_n^{(j)}\rangle=\delta_{ij}.
\]

## 4A) Key idea: the perturbation selects the proper linear combinations
The real eigenstates (order 0 in \(\lambda\)) is not a specific \(|\phi_n^{(i)}\rangle\), but a combination:
\[
|\phi_{n,a}^{(0)}\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{r_n}\alpha_i^{(a)}|\phi_n^{(i)}\rangle,
\qquad a=1,\dots,r_n.
\]

Expand:
\[
E_{n,a}=E_n^{(0)}+\lambda E_{n,a}^{(1)}+\lambda^2E_{n,a}^{(2)}+\dots
\]
\[
|\Psi_{n,a}\rangle = |\phi_{n,a}^{(0)}\rangle+\lambda|\Psi_{n,a}^{(1)}\rangle+\dots
\]

## 4B) Order \(\lambda^1\): derivation of the homogeneous secular system
Reconciliating the same passage of order 1:
\[
(H_0-E_n^{(0)})|\Psi_{n,a}^{(1)}\rangle + (H'-E_{n,a}^{(1)})|\phi_{n,a}^{(0)}\rangle=0.
\]

Projection on \(\langle \phi_n^{(j)}|\) (which is in the degenerate subspace): the first term gives zero, then:
\[
\langle \phi_n^{(j)}|H'|\phi_{n,a}^{(0)}\rangle
- E_{n,a}^{(1)}\langle \phi_n^{(j)}|\phi_{n,a}^{(0)}\rangle =0.
\]

But
\[
\langle \phi_n^{(j)}|H'|\phi_{n,a}^{(0)}\rangle
=\sum_{i=1}^{r_n}\alpha_i^{(a)}\langle \phi_n^{(j)}|H'|\phi_n^{(i)}\rangle
=\sum_{i=1}^{r_n}H'_{ji}\alpha_i^{(a)},
\]
e
\[
\langle \phi_n^{(j)}|\phi_{n,a}^{(0)}\rangle=\alpha_j^{(a)}.
\]

So I get the system:
\[
\boxed{
\sum_{i=1}^{r_n}\big(H'_{ji}-E_{n,a}^{(1)}\delta_{ji}\big)\,\alpha_i^{(a)}=0,
\qquad j=1,\dots,r_n.}
\]

To have non-trivial solutions \(\alpha^{(a)}\neq 0\) must be:
\[
\boxed{\det\!\big(H'_{ij}-E^{(1)}\delta_{ij}\big)=0.}
\]
The \(r_n\) solutions \(E^{(1)}\) are **eigenvalues** of the \(H'\) matrix restricted to \(S_n\); the corresponding autovectors give the coefficients \(\alpha_i^{(a)}\).

*In practice:* “in the degenerate subspace, diagonally \(H'\)”.

## 4C) Corrections outside of subspace (as in non-degenerate)
Selected the "good" eigenstates \(|\phi_{n,a}^{(0)}\rangle\), I can write:
\[
\boxed{|\Psi_{n,a}^{(1)}\rangle=\sum_{m\notin S_n}\frac{\langle \phi_m|H'|\phi_{n,a}^{(0)}\rangle}{E_n^{(0)}-E_m^{(0)}}\,|\phi_m\rangle}
\]
e
\[
\boxed{E_{n,a}^{(2)}=\sum_{m\notin S_n}\frac{|\langle \phi_m|H'|\phi_{n,a}^{(0)}\rangle|^2}{E_n^{(0)}-E_m^{(0)}}.}
\]

---

# 5) Example \(r_n=2\): splitting and mixing
In degenerate subspace with base \(\{|\phi_n^{(1)}\rangle,|\phi_n^{(2)}\rangle\}\), the matrix is:
\[
H'^{(S_n)}=
\begin{pmatrix}
H'_{11} & H'_{12}\\
H'_{21} & H'_{22}
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad H'_{21}=H_{12}^{\prime *}.
\]

Secular equation:
\[
\det\begin{pmatrix}
H'_{11}-E^{(1)} & H'_{12}\\
H'_{21} & H'_{22}-E^{(1)}
\end{pmatrix}=0.
\]

Solution:
\[
\boxed{E_\pm^{(1)}=\frac{H'_{11}+H'_{22}}{2}\pm\frac12\sqrt{(H'_{11}-H'_{22})^2+4|H'_{12}|^2}}.
\]
Interpreting: the level is **scakes** into two separate layers of a root proportional amount.

If \(H'_{12}=0\) (no coupling between the two states), then:
\[
E_+^{(1)}=H'_{11},\qquad E_-^{(1)}=H'_{22},
\]
and the "good" eigenstates coincide with those chosen at the beginning (no mixing).

---

# Symbols dictionary (to know each by one)
- \(H_0\): Hamiltonian not disturbed (note, solvable exactly).
- \(H'\): time-independent perturbations (small operator compared to \(H_0\)).
- \(\lambda\): dimension parameter “Count” (\(\lambda\ll 1\)); at the end of the day, \(\lambda=1\).
- \(|\phi_n\rangle\): \(H_0\) eigenstates.
- \(E_n^{(0)}\): unperturbed energies.
- \(|\Psi_n\rangle\): \(H\) (perturbed) eigenstates.
- \(E_n\): disturbed energies.
- \(E_n^{(1)},E_n^{(2)}\): corrections to I and II order.
- \(H'_{mn}=\langle \phi_m|H'|\phi_n\rangle\): perturbation matrix elements in \(H_0\) base.
- \(c_{nm}^{(1)}\): \(|\Psi_n^{(1)}\rangle\) expansion coefficients based on \(|\phi_m\rangle\).
- \(r_n\): Layer degeneracy order \(E_n^{(0)}\).
- \(S_n\): Degenerous subspace associated with \(E_n^{(0)}\).
- \(H'^{(S_n)}\): \(H'\) subspace restricted matrix \(S_n\).
- “secular equation”: \(\det(H'_{ij}-E^{(1)}\delta_{ij})=0\).

---

# Physical meaning (to be said well)
- The perturbation is mixed with the eigenstates of \(H_0\): the exact states no longer coincide with those not disturbed, but are linear combinations.
- \(E_n^{(1)}=\langle H'\rangle\) is the medium shift of the unperturbed state perturbation.
- \(E_n^{(2)}\) describes the effect of mixing with other layers: the denominator \(E_n^{(0)}-E_m^{(0)}\) appears.  
  In particular, it is related to **repulsion of ** layers: neighbouring layers tend to drift away.
- In the presence of degeneracy, the perturbation eliminates (usually) degeneracy: the good statuses are those that diagonalize \(H'\) in the subspace.

---

All order \(\lambda\) get \((H_0-E_n^{(0)})|\Psi_n^{(1)}\rangle+(H'-E_n^{(1)})|\phi_n\rangle=0\). Projecting on \(\langle \phi_n|\) Derivation of \(E_n^{(1)}=\langle \phi_n|H'|\phi_n\rangle\). Expanding \(|\Psi_n^{(1)}\rangle=\sum_{m\neq n}c_{nm}^{(1)}|\phi_m\rangle\) and projecting on \(\langle \phi_m|\) with \(m\neq n\) find \(c_{nm}^{(1)}=\langle \phi_m|H'|\phi_n\rangle/(E_n^{(0)}-E_m^{(0)})\).

If there is degeneracy, before diagonalizing \(H'\) in degenerate subspace: Solve \(\det(H'_{ij}-E^{(1)}\delta_{ij})=0\), eigenvalues give shift to first order and autovectors give combinations of "good" states that eliminate degeneracy.

# DIM 8 - Addition of angular momenta \( \vec J=\vec J_1+\vec J_2 \)
**(ICOC/CSCO, possible values of \(j\), build with ladder, Clebsch-Gordan coefficients, example \( \tfrac12+\tfrac12 \))**

- **Core (sum of angular momenta, coupled base, CG):** P2·23A `img/208.jpg`-`img/214.jpg`  
- **Example 2 fermions / symmetries (useful for triplet/singlet):** P2·23B `img/214.jpg`-`img/215.jpg`  
- **Support (algebra of \(L\), ladder, switchers):** P1·17A/B `img/147.jpg`-`img/165.jpg`  
- **Spin 1/2 support (Pauli/Stern-Gerlach):** P1·21B `img/188.jpg`-`img/199.jpg`

# 0) Target boss (what you need to be able to prove and say)
1) Define the sum:
\[
\vec J=\vec J_1+\vec J_2,\qquad J_z=J_{1z}+J_{2z},\qquad J_\pm=J_{1\pm}+J_{2\pm}.
\]

2) Say which is the natural (l)**ICOC/CSCO** and why:
- base **not coupled** (uncoupled): \(|j_1 m_1\rangle|j_2 m_2\rangle\)
- base **coupled** (coupled): \(|j_1 j_2; j m\rangle\)

3) Demonstrate **possible values of total**:
\[
\boxed{j = j_1+j_2,\; j_1+j_2-1,\; \dots,\; |j_1-j_2|}
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\boxed{m=-j,-j+1,\dots,j}
\]
(with degeneracy \(2j+1\)).

4) Write the transformation between bases with **Clebsch-Gordan**:
\[
\boxed{|j_1 j_2; j m\rangle=\sum_{m_1,m_2} C^{\,j m}_{j_1 m_1\, j_2 m_2}\,|j_1 m_1\rangle|j_2 m_2\rangle}
\]
with constraint
\[
\boxed{m=m_1+m_2.}
\]

5) Give the key example:
\[
\boxed{\tfrac12\oplus\tfrac12 = 1 \oplus 0}
\]
with triplet and explicit singlet.

---

# 1) Essential algebra (the toolbox)
Each angular momenta \(\vec J\) meets:
\[
[J_x,J_y]=i\hbar J_z\quad \text{(and cyclical)}.
\]

I define:
\[
J_\pm = J_x \pm iJ_y.
\]

Standard reports:
\[
[J_z,J_\pm]=\pm \hbar J_\pm,
\qquad
[J^2,J_z]=0,
\qquad
[J^2,J_\pm]=0.
\]

eigenstates of \(J^2\) and \(J_z\):
\[
J^2|j m\rangle=\hbar^2 j(j+1)|j m\rangle,
\qquad
J_z|j m\rangle=\hbar m|j m\rangle.
\]

Ladder action:
\[
J_\pm|j m\rangle=\hbar\sqrt{j(j+1)-m(m\pm1)}\,|j,m\pm1\rangle.
\]

---

# 2) Two angular momenta: which operators commute
You have two independent systems with \(\vec J_1,\vec J_2\). Different system components ** switch**:
\[
[J_{1a},J_{2b}]=0\quad \forall a,b\in\{x,y,z\}.
\]

I define the total:
\[
\vec J=\vec J_1+\vec J_2.
\]

So:
\[
J^2=(\vec J_1+\vec J_2)^2=J_1^2+J_2^2+2\,\vec J_1\cdot\vec J_2.
\]

**ICOC/CSCO typical:**
- **Uncoupled base:** \(\{J_1^2,J_{1z},J_2^2,J_{2z}\}\)  
  \(|j_1 m_1\rangle|j_2 m_2\rangle\)
- **Coupled base:** \(\{J_1^2,J_2^2,J^2,J_z\}\)  
  \(|j_1 j_2; j m\rangle\)

Why does it work?
\[
[J_1^2,J^2]=[J_2^2,J^2]=0,\qquad [J^2,J_z]=0
\]
and \(J_1^2, J_2^2\) fix the type of moments you are adding up.

---

# 3) Demonstration of possible values of \(m\)
This is the easiest part of the boss test.

Non-coupled base:
\[
J_{1z}|j_1 m_1\rangle=\hbar m_1|j_1 m_1\rangle,\quad
J_{2z}|j_2 m_2\rangle=\hbar m_2|j_2 m_2\rangle.
\]

On the product:
\[
J_z\,(|j_1 m_1\rangle|j_2 m_2\rangle)
=(J_{1z}+J_{2z})|j_1 m_1\rangle|j_2 m_2\rangle
=\hbar(m_1+m_2)|j_1 m_1\rangle|j_2 m_2\rangle.
\]

So:
\[
\boxed{m=m_1+m_2.}
\]

And since:
\[
m_1\in[-j_1,j_1],\quad m_2\in[-j_2,j_2]
\]
the maximum \(m\) obtainable is:
\[
m_{\max}=j_1+j_2
\]
and the minimum:
\[
m_{\min}=-(j_1+j_2).
\]

---

# 4) Demonstration of possible values of \(j\)
This is where the standard clipboard demonstration uses: **maximum weight state + ladder + size count**.

## 4A) There is a state with \(m=j_1+j_2\)
Get the state:
\[
|j_1,j_1\rangle|j_2,j_2\rangle.
\]
Ha:
\[
m=j_1+j_2.
\]

In any representation of \(SU(2)\), a state with \(m\) maximum is annihilated by \(J_+\). We check directly:
\[
J_+=J_{1+}+J_{2+}.
\]
Ma:
\[
J_{1+}|j_1,j_1\rangle=0,\qquad J_{2+}|j_2,j_2\rangle=0
\]
then:
\[
J_+|j_1,j_1\rangle|j_2,j_2\rangle=0.
\]

So this is a **candidate state of maximum weight of ** for some total representation with \(j=m_{\max}\).  
So it has to be:
\[
\boxed{j_{\max}=j_1+j_2.}
\]

## 4B) Generate a complete multiplet with \(J_-\)
Applying \(J_- = J_{1-}+J_{2-}\) repeatedly, son-in-law states with:
\[
m=j_1+j_2,\; j_1+j_2-1,\; \dots,\; -(j_1+j_2)
\]
and then a multiple with:
\[
2(j_1+j_2)+1
\]
States.

These (normalized) states form the multiplet with:
\[
\boxed{j=j_1+j_2.}
\]

## 4C) And the others \(j\)? (idea: maximum multiplets and repeats)
The total space has size:
\[
(2j_1+1)(2j_2+1).
\]

Without multiple \(j=j_1+j_2\) having \(2(j_1+j_2)+1\) states, a space with size remains:
\[
(2j_1+1)(2j_2+1)-\big(2(j_1+j_2)+1\big),
\]
which still decomposes into multiplets of \(SU(2)\). The next maximum \(m\) remaining is \(j_1+j_2-1\), so the next \(j\) is:
\[
j=j_1+j_2-1.
\]

Repeating, you get the chain:
\[
\boxed{j=j_1+j_2,\; j_1+j_2-1,\;\dots\;}
\]

The process stops when you reach the minimum \(j\) possible compatible with the size and existence of the states: the minimum is:
\[
\boxed{j_{\min}=|j_1-j_2|.}
\]

## 4D) Dimensional control (super elegant, to say if they ask you why just up to \(|j_1-j_2|\)?
It shall apply to:
\[
\boxed{(2j_1+1)(2j_2+1)=\sum_{j=|j_1-j_2|}^{j_1+j_2}(2j+1)}
\]
which is true (sum of multiplettes that fill all the space).  
This closes the demonstration of completeness and the values allowed.

# 5) Clebsch-Gordan coefficients (transformation between bases)
You have two complete bases:

### Uncoupled base
\[
|j_1 m_1\rangle|j_2 m_2\rangle
\quad\text{(diagonalizza }J_{1z},J_{2z}\text{)}
\]

### Coupled base
\[
|j_1 j_2; j m\rangle
\quad\text{(diagonalizza }J^2,J_z\text{)}
\]

The transformation is a unitary basic change:
\[
\boxed{
|j_1 j_2; j m\rangle=\sum_{m_1,m_2}
C^{\,j m}_{j_1 m_1\, j_2 m_2}\;
|j_1 m_1\rangle|j_2 m_2\rangle
}
\]

**Required selection rule:**
\[
\boxed{C^{jm}_{j_1m_1j_2m_2}\neq0 \ \Rightarrow\  m=m_1+m_2.}
\]

**Ortonormality (useful if asked):**
\[
\sum_{m_1,m_2} C^{jm}_{j_1m_1j_2m_2}\, C^{j'm'}_{j_1m_1j_2m_2}=\delta_{jj'}\delta_{mm'}.
\]

**Physical interpretation:** \(C\) are amplitudes (complex numbers, often real in standard conventions) that say 'how much' a total state \(j,m\) is made from combinations of the two \(m_1,m_2\).

---

# 6) Basic example: \(j_1=\tfrac12\), \(j_2=\tfrac12\)
This is the example you must know how to act perfect.

## 6A) Uncoupled base (spin up/down)
Identifying:
\[
\left|\tfrac12,+\tfrac12\right\rangle \equiv |\uparrow\rangle,\qquad
\left|\tfrac12,-\tfrac12\right\rangle \equiv |\downarrow\rangle.
\]

The possible products are 4:
\[
|\uparrow\uparrow\rangle,\quad
|\uparrow\downarrow\rangle,\quad
|\downarrow\uparrow\rangle,\quad
|\downarrow\downarrow\rangle.
\]

Size: \((2\cdot\tfrac12+1)^2=2^2=4\).

## 6B) Possible values of \(j\)
\[
j = j_1+j_2=1,\quad \text{oppure}\quad j=|j_1-j_2|=0.
\]
So:
\[
\boxed{\tfrac12\oplus\tfrac12 = 1\oplus 0}
\]
That is, a triplet (\(j=1\), 3 states) + a singlet (\(j=0\), 1 state).

## 6C) Explanatory construction with ladder (more demonstration method)
### Maximum state \(m\)
Maximum \(m=m_1+m_2=+\tfrac12+\tfrac12=+1\).
The only product that makes it is:
\[
\boxed{|1,1\rangle = |\uparrow\uparrow\rangle.}
\]

### Apply \(J_-\) to get \(|1,0\rangle\)
\[
J_- = J_{1-}+J_{2-}.
\]

I act on \(|\uparrow\uparrow\rangle\):
- \(J_{1-}|\uparrow\uparrow\rangle \propto |\downarrow\uparrow\rangle\)
- \(J_{2-}|\uparrow\uparrow\rangle \propto |\uparrow\downarrow\rangle\)

So:
\[
J_-|\uparrow\uparrow\rangle \propto |\downarrow\uparrow\rangle+|\uparrow\downarrow\rangle.
\]

I normalize and define:
\[
\boxed{|1,0\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\big(|\uparrow\downarrow\rangle+|\downarrow\uparrow\rangle\big).}
\]

### Apply again \(J_-\) for \(|1,-1\rangle\)
By symmetry:
\[
\boxed{|1,-1\rangle=|\downarrow\downarrow\rangle.}
\]

### The remaining state must be orthogonal: the singlet \(|0,0\rangle\)
Between the two states with \(m=0\), i.e. \(|\uparrow\downarrow\rangle\) and \(|\downarrow\uparrow\rangle\), we have already used the symmetrical combination for \(|1,0\rangle\).  
The only orthogonal combination is the antisymmetrical:

\[
\boxed{|0,0\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\big(|\uparrow\downarrow\rangle-|\downarrow\uparrow\rangle\big).}
\]

End of construction.

# DIM 9 - Identical particles: exchange operator, symmetrization/antisymmetrization, \([P,H]=0\), Pauli/Slater, singlet-triplet relation ↔ even/odd \(\ell\)

## where it appears in the notes
- **Core:** P2·24 = identical parts → `img/215.jpg`-`img/220.jpg`  
- **Useful support (spin 1/2 triplet/singlet):** P2·23B `img/214.jpg`-`img/215.jpg` (the part that fits with DIM 8)

# 0) Objectives
Demonstrate and know how to say well:

1) Define the **exchange operator** \( \hat P_{12} \) and its properties:
\[
\hat P_{12}^2=\hat I,\qquad \hat P_{12}^\dagger=\hat P_{12},\qquad \text{eigenvalues } \pm 1.
\]

2) For **identical particles**: Hamiltonian is invariant by exchange ⇒
\[
\hat P_{12}\hat H\hat P_{12}=\hat H\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad [\hat H,\hat P_{12}]=0.
\]

3) If \([H,P]=0\), then I can choose common eigenstates ⇒ the physical states separate into:
- **symmetric**: \( \hat P_{12}|\psi\rangle=+|\psi\rangle\) (bosons)
- **Symmetrical anti-anti-symmetry**: \( \hat P_{12}|\psi\rangle=-|\psi\rangle\) (fermions)

4) Build symmetric/antisymmetric states from \(|a\rangle|b\rangle\) products and get to:
- **Pauli principle** (if \(a=b\) Fermion status is zero)
- **Slater Determinant**

5) Connect with spin 1/2 (DIM 8): triplet/singlet + symmetry of the spatial part.  
And say the typical rule:
\[
\text{singoletto} \Rightarrow \text{symmetric space} \Rightarrow \ell \ \text{pari}
\]
\[
\text{tripletto} \Rightarrow \text{antisymmetrical space} \Rightarrow \ell \ \text{dispari}
\]
(where \(\ell\) is the orbital angular momentum of relative motion).

---

# 1) Definition of the exchange operator \( \hat P_{12} \)
I consider two particles and one state in full representation (position + spin):
\[
x_1\equiv(\vec r_1,\sigma_1),\qquad x_2\equiv(\vec r_2,\sigma_2).
\]
The two-body wave function is:
\[
\psi(x_1,x_2)=\langle x_1,x_2|\psi\rangle.
\]

**Defining** l-exchanger \( \hat P_{12} \) as the one exchanging labels 1 and 2:
\[
(\hat P_{12}\psi)(x_1,x_2)=\psi(x_2,x_1).
\]

---

# 2) Key properties of \( \hat P_{12} \)
## 2A) \( \hat P_{12}^2=\hat I \)
Exchange twice:
\[
(\hat P_{12}^2\psi)(x_1,x_2)=\hat P_{12}\big(\psi(x_2,x_1)\big)=\psi(x_1,x_2).
\]
So:
\[
\boxed{\hat P_{12}^2=\hat I.}
\]

## 2B) \( \hat P_{12}^\dagger=\hat P_{12} \) and unitarity
From the exchange does not change the norm (it is only a permutation of variables):
\[
\int |(\hat P_{12}\psi)(x_1,x_2)|^2\,dx_1dx_2
=\int |\psi(x_2,x_1)|^2\,dx_1dx_2
=\int |\psi(x_1,x_2)|^2\,dx_1dx_2.
\]
So \(P\) is **unitary**: \(P^\dagger P=I\).  
Plus, with \(P^2=I\) follows \(P^{-1}=P\). But for a unit \(P^{-1}=P^\dagger\), then:
\[
\boxed{\hat P_{12}^\dagger=\hat P_{12}.}
\]
So \(P\) is also **hermitian**.

## 2C) Values of \( \hat P_{12} \) are \(\pm 1\)
If \(\hat P_{12}|\psi\rangle = \lambda |\psi\rangle\), I apply \(P\) again:
\[
\hat P_{12}^2|\psi\rangle=\lambda^2|\psi\rangle.
\]
But \(P^2=I\), then:
\[
|\psi\rangle=\lambda^2|\psi\rangle \Rightarrow \lambda^2=1 \Rightarrow \boxed{\lambda=\pm 1.}
\]

Thus the states are divided into **symmetric** (+) and **antisymmetric** (−).

---

# 3) For identical particles: because \([H,P]=0\)
For two identical particles (same mass, same charge, etc.) the Hamiltonian has the same shape for 1 and 2. A typical model is:
\[
\hat H=\underbrace{\frac{\hat p_1^2}{2m}+V(\hat r_1)}_{\hat h(1)}
+\underbrace{\frac{\hat p_2^2}{2m}+V(\hat r_2)}_{\hat h(2)}
+\underbrace{U(|\hat r_1-\hat r_2|)}_{\text{symmetric interaction}}.
\]

If exchange \(1\leftrightarrow 2\):
- \(\hat p_1^2 \leftrightarrow \hat p_2^2\)
- \(V(\hat r_1)\leftrightarrow V(\hat r_2)\)
- \(U(|r_1-r_2|)\) remains the same because it depends only on distance.

So:
\[
\boxed{\hat P_{12}\hat H\hat P_{12}=\hat H.}
\]
Multiplying right by \(\hat P_{12}\) and using \(\hat P_{12}^2=I\):
\[
\hat P_{12}\hat H=\hat H\hat P_{12}
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
\boxed{[\hat H,\hat P_{12}]=0.}
\]

**Physical interpretation:** If the particles are really identical, the particles can't change any measurable prediction ⇒ \(H\) must respect this symmetry.

---

# 4) Consequences: common eigenstates ⇒ symmetry of the wave function
If \([H,P]=0\), then \(H\) and \(P\) are **compatible** (DIM 3): I can choose a common eigenstates base.

So I can take the energy eigenstates so that they are also exchange eigenstates:
\[
\hat H|\psi\rangle=E|\psi\rangle,\qquad \hat P_{12}|\psi\rangle=\pm|\psi\rangle.
\]

Therefore, for identical particles, energy states (and in general those physically admitted) can be chosen **symmetric ** or **antisymmetric**.

---

# 5) Symmetrization postulate (bosons vs fermions)
At this point the physical data (spin-statistics) enters:
- **Bosons (whole spin)**: total state must be **symmetric**
  \[
  \boxed{\hat P_{12}|\psi\rangle=+|\psi\rangle}
  \]
- **fermions (semi-whole spin)**: total state must be **antisymmetric**
  \[
  \boxed{\hat P_{12}|\psi\rangle=-|\psi\rangle}
  \]

(In non-relativistic MQ courses it is often taken as a postulate; the deep origin is relativistic/QFT.)

---

# 6) Express construction: symmetrizer and antisymmetrizer
I define the projectors:
\[
\boxed{\hat S=\frac{1+\hat P_{12}}{2}},\qquad
\boxed{\hat A=\frac{1-\hat P_{12}}{2}}.
\]
Properties to remember:
\[
\hat S^2=\hat S,\quad \hat A^2=\hat A,\quad \hat S\hat A=0,\quad \hat S+\hat A=I.
\]
Then \(S\) project into the symmetrical space, \(A\) into the antisymmetric space.

---

## 6A) From product states to symmetric/antisymmetric states
I take two states to a particle \(|a\rangle\), \(|b\rangle\). The product is \(|a\rangle_1|b\rangle_2\).

- State **symmetric**:
\[
\boxed{|a,b\rangle_+ = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\big(|a\rangle_1|b\rangle_2+|b\rangle_1|a\rangle_2\big)}
\]
- State **antisymmetric**:
\[
\boxed{|a,b\rangle_- = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\big(|a\rangle_1|b\rangle_2-|b\rangle_1|a\rangle_2\big)}
\]

(This form is perfect if \(|a\rangle\) and \(|b\rangle\) are orthonormal; otherwise normalization changes slightly, but the idea remains identical.)

---

# 7) Pauli principle and Slater determinant
## 7A) Pauli in a row (dry demonstration)
If I take two fermions in **same** state \(|a\rangle\) (i.e. \(a=b\)), then:
\[
|a,a\rangle_-=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|a\rangle_1|a\rangle_2-|a\rangle_1|a\rangle_2)=0.
\]
So an antisymmetric physical state ** does not exist** if two fermions occupy the same state at a particle.

\[
\boxed{\text{Two fermions cannot occupy the same quantum state.}}
\]

## 7B) Slater (general form for \(N\) fermions)
For \(N\) stops in orbital \(\phi_1,\dots,\phi_N\), the antisymmetrical state is the **determinant of Slater**:
\[
\boxed{
\Psi(x_1,\dots,x_N)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N!}}
\begin{vmatrix}
\phi_1(x_1)&\phi_2(x_1)&\cdots&\phi_N(x_1)\\
\phi_1(x_2)&\phi_2(x_2)&\cdots&\phi_N(x_2)\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\phi_1(x_N)&\phi_2(x_N)&\cdots&\phi_N(x_N)
\end{vmatrix}
}
\]
If two orbitals coincide (two equal columns), the determining factor is zero ⇒ Pauli.

---

# 8) Connection to DIM 8: two spin-\(1/2\) particles, singlet/triplet and total symmetry
For two electrons (fermions, spin \(1/2\)) **total state** must be antisymmetric:
\[
\boxed{\Psi_{\text{tot}}=\Psi_{\text{spaz}}(\vec r_1,\vec r_2)\,\chi_{\text{spin}}(\sigma_1,\sigma_2)}
\]
and must be:
\[
\hat P_{12}\Psi_{\text{tot}} = -\Psi_{\text{tot}}.
\]

From DIM 8 you know:
- **Triplet ** (\(S=1\)) is **symmetric** in the spin:
  \[
  \chi_{S=1}\ \text{simmetrica}
  \]
- **Singulet** (\(S=0\)) is **antisymmetric** in spin:
  \[
  \chi_{S=0}\ \text{antisimmetrica}
  \]

So to have total antisymmetric:

### Case 1: singlet spin (antisymmetric spin)
Space is **symmetric**:
\[
(-)\times(+)=(-)
\]
\[
\boxed{\chi_{S=0}\ \Rightarrow\ \Psi_{\text{spaz}} \ \text{simmetrica}}
\]

### Case 2: triplet spin (symmetric spin)
Space Server **antisymmetric**:
\[
(+)\times(-)=(-)
\]
\[
\boxed{\chi_{S=1}\ \Rightarrow\ \Psi_{\text{spaz}} \ \text{antisimmetrica}}
\]

# 9) Even/odd \(\ell\) rule (exchange ↔ \(\vec r \to -\vec r\) in relative motion)
For two particles it is useful to switch to:
- mass center coordinate \(\vec R\)
- relative coordinate \(\vec r=\vec r_1-\vec r_2\)

Exchange \(1\leftrightarrow 2\) leaves \(\vec R\) unchanged but sends:
\[
\vec r \to -\vec r.
\]

If the angular part of the relative motion is \(Y_{\ell m}(\hat r)\), the property shall be:
\[
Y_{\ell m}(-\hat r)=(-1)^\ell\,Y_{\ell m}(\hat r).
\]

So:
- if \(\ell\) is **pari**, the space part is **symmetric** under exchange
- if \(\ell\) is **odd**, the space part is **antisymmetric** under exchange

Combining point 8:

\[
\boxed{\text{singoletto }(S=0)\ \Rightarrow\ \Psi_{\text{spaz}} \text{ simmetrica } \Rightarrow\ \ell\ \text{pari}}
\]
\[
\boxed{\text{tripletto }(S=1)\ \Rightarrow\ \Psi_{\text{spaz}} \text{ antisimmetrica } \Rightarrow\ \ell\ \text{dispari}}
\]

This is a very typical phrase to be examined.

# DIM 10 - Hellmann-Feynman Theorem + spectroscopic notation

## where it appears in the notes
- **Core (Hellmann-Feynman + spectroscopic notation):** P2·27 → `img/236.jpg`-`img/237.jpg` (pages 236-237)

---

# 0) Objectives
Prove that if a Hamiltonian depends on a \(\lambda\) parameter,
\[
\hat H(\lambda)\,|n(\lambda)\rangle = E_n(\lambda)\,|n(\lambda)\rangle,
\]
then (for normalized eigenstates and non-degenerate case):
\[
\boxed{\;\frac{dE_n}{d\lambda}=\left\langle n(\lambda)\left|\frac{\partial \hat H}{\partial \lambda}\right|n(\lambda)\right\rangle\;}
\]
and then be able to read/describe **spectroscopic notation** (type \(n\ell_j\) and/or \(^{2S+1}L_J\), parity, etc.).

---

# 1) Complete demonstration of Hellmann-Feynman's theorem

## 1A) Hypothesis
- \(\hat H(\lambda)\) is **hermitian** (observable energy).
- \(E_n(\lambda)\) is an eigenvalues (non-degenerate, for the clean version)
- \(|n(\lambda)\rangle\) is eigenstates **normalised**:
  \[
  \langle n(\lambda)|n(\lambda)\rangle=1.
  \]

## 1B) Parts from the equation to the values and derivation
\[
\hat H(\lambda)|n(\lambda)\rangle = E_n(\lambda)|n(\lambda)\rangle.
\]

Deviation from \(\lambda\) (total output):
\[
\frac{\partial \hat H}{\partial \lambda}|n\rangle + \hat H\,\frac{\partial |n\rangle}{\partial \lambda}
=
\frac{dE_n}{d\lambda}|n\rangle + E_n\,\frac{\partial |n\rangle}{\partial \lambda}.
\]
(here I abbreviated \(|n\rangle = |n(\lambda)\rangle\), \(E_n=E_n(\lambda)\)).

## 1C) Multiply left by \(\langle n|\)
\[
\langle n|\frac{\partial \hat H}{\partial \lambda}|n\rangle
+
\langle n|\hat H|n'\rangle
=
\frac{dE_n}{d\lambda}\langle n|n\rangle
+
E_n\langle n|n'\rangle
\]
where \(|n'\rangle \equiv \frac{\partial |n\rangle}{\partial \lambda}\).

Now I use two facts:

### (i) Normalization
\[
\langle n|n\rangle=1.
\]

### (ii) Hermiticity + eigenvalues
From \(\hat H|n\rangle = E_n|n\rangle\) and \(\hat H=\hat H^\dagger\) also follows:
\[
\langle n|\hat H = E_n \langle n|.
\]
So:
\[
\langle n|\hat H|n'\rangle = E_n \langle n|n'\rangle.
\]

Replace: the terms \(E_n\langle n|n'\rangle\) are deleted between LHS and RHS, and remains:

\[
\boxed{\;\frac{dE_n}{d\lambda}=\langle n|\frac{\partial \hat H}{\partial \lambda}|n\rangle\;}
\]

The demonstration is over.

---

# 2) Important comments (which often ask)
## 2A) Normalization doesn't bother you.
Even if you do not impose \(\langle n|n'\rangle=0\), the theorem remains true: the deletion takes place anyway.

Coming \(\langle n|n\rangle=1\):
\[
\frac{d}{d\lambda}\langle n|n\rangle=\langle n'|n\rangle+\langle n|n'\rangle=0
\]
⇒ \(\langle n|n'\rangle\) is purely imaginary, but it does not need to be fixed: Hellmann-Feynman does not depend on this phase choice.

## 2B) Degenerate case (what to say without bogged down)
If \(E_n\) is degenerate, within the degenerate subspace the eigenstates \(|n\rangle\) is not unique: you can mix the eigenstates linearly.

The correct sentence is:
> If there is degeneracy, to apply Hellmann-Feynman to a single layer you must choose a degenerate subspace base that **diagonalize** the \(\langle i|\partial_\lambda H|j\rangle\) matrix within that subspace.

It is the same spirit as the degenerate perturbation (DIM 7): first diagonalize within the degeneracy, then everything returns to clean.

# 3) Physical meaning (the true point of the theorem)
Hellmann-Feynman says:

- If \(\lambda\) is a physical parameter of the problem (external field, nuclear distance, nuclear charge \(Z\), etc.), then **the energy derivative** compared to that parameter is calculated as **expectation value of the Hamiltonian derivative**.

### Typical examples to say
1) If \(\lambda = R\) is a nuclear coordinate (molecules, Born-Oppenheimer):
\[
F_R = -\frac{dE}{dR} = -\left\langle \frac{\partial \hat H}{\partial R}\right\rangle
\]
⇒ is the basis for calculating **forces** on atoms.

2) If \(\hat H(\lambda)=\hat H_0+\lambda \hat V\):
\[
\frac{dE_n}{d\lambda}=\langle \hat V\rangle
\]
and by \(\lambda\) small leads you immediately to the perturbation of the first order:
\[
E_n(\lambda)\approx E_n(0)+\lambda\langle n|\hat V|n\rangle
\]
(it is DIM 7 view as derivative)

# 4) Spectroscopic notation (the one you need to read on the fly)

2) **multi-electronic atom (LS coupling)**: \(^{2S+1}L_J\) + parity

## 4A) Notation \(n\ell_j\) (very common)
- \(n\) = main quantum number.
- \(\ell\) = angular orbital moment, written by letters:
  - \(\ell=0 \to s\)
  - \(\ell=1 \to p\)
  - \(\ell=2 \to d\)
  - \(\ell=3 \to f\)
  - \(\ell=4 \to g\), \(\ell=5 \to h\),...

\]

Examples:
- \(2p_{3/2}\): \(n=2\), \(\ell=1\), \(j=3/2\).
- \(1s_{1/2}\): \(n=1\), \(\ell=0\), \(j=1/2\).

### Letters by \(L\)

- \(L=2 \to D\)
- \(L=3 \to F\)
- \(L=4 \to G\)
- \(L=5 \to H\)
- \(L=6 \to I\)
(... continue)

### Possible values of \(J\)

### Equality
Often you will find a type symbol \(^{2S+1}L_J^{\circ}\) (or \(^{o}\)) for ** odd parity** (odd).  
The equality of electronic configuration shall be:
\[
\Pi = (-1)^{\sum_i \ell_i}.
\]
- if \(\sum \ell_i\) is equal → parity **pari** (often nothing is written)
- if \(\sum \ell_i\) is odd → parity **odd** (does \(^{\circ}\) or \(o\))

### Quick reading example
\(^{3}P_{2}\):
- \(2S+1=3 \Rightarrow S=1\) (triplet),

# 6) Oral script (90-120 sec)
I consider a Hamiltonian \(H(\lambda)\) Hermitian with normalized eigenstates \(H(\lambda)|n(\lambda)\rangle=E_n(\lambda)|n(\lambda)\rangle\). Devious to \(\lambda\): \((\partial_\lambda H)|n\rangle+H|n'\rangle=E_n'|n\rangle+E_n|n'\rangle\). Multiply left to \(\langle n|\): \(\langle n|\partial_\lambda H|n\rangle+\langle n|H|n'\rangle=E_n'\langle n|n\rangle+E_n\langle n|n'\rangle\). Ma \(\langle n|n\rangle=1\) and, using \(H\) Hermitian, \(\langle n|H=E_n\langle n|\), then \(\langle n|H|n'\rangle=E_n\langle n|n'\rangle\) and the two terms are deleted. Stay \(E_n'=\langle n|\partial_\lambda H|n\rangle\): this is Hellmann-Feynman. Physically means that the energy change over a parameter is calculated as the expectation value of the Hamiltonian derivative; e.g. for a geometric parameter \(R\) the force is \(F=-dE/dR=-\langle\partial_R H\rangle\). # 1) Setup: non-relativistic Hamiltonian and degeneracies
Basic Hamiltonian (hydrogenide, not relativistic):
\[
\boxed{
H_0=\frac{\hat p^2}{2m}+V(r)
=
\frac{\hat p^2}{2m}-\frac{Ze^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{1}{r}
}
\]

Typical (no spin-orbit) eigenstates:
\[
|n\ell m_\ell\rangle\otimes|m_s\rangle
\]
with energy:
\[
\boxed{E_n^{(0)}=-\frac{mc^2(Z\alpha)^2}{2n^2}}
\]
and degeneracy (without spin): \(n^2\). With spin \(1/2\): \(2n^2\).

The fine structure is an effect of order \((v/c)^2\sim (Z\alpha)^2\), so energies change in order \((Z\alpha)^4mc^2\): very small compared to \(E_n^{(0)}\).

---

# 2) Where the three terms \(H_{MV},H_{SO},H_D\) come from

## 2A) Mass-velocity correction \(H_{MV}\)
Departure from free relativistic energy:
\[
E = \sqrt{p^2c^2+m^2c^4}.
\]
I remove rest energy \(mc^2\) to obtain relativistic kinetic energy:
\[
T_{rel}= \sqrt{p^2c^2+m^2c^4}-mc^2
= mc^2\left(\sqrt{1+\frac{p^2}{m^2c^2}}-1\right).
\]
Development for \(\frac{p^2}{m^2c^2}\ll1\):
\[
\sqrt{1+x}\approx 1+\frac{x}{2}-\frac{x^2}{8}+\cdots
\]
with \(x=\frac{p^2}{m^2c^2}\).
\[
T_{rel}\approx mc^2\left(\frac{1}{2}\frac{p^2}{m^2c^2}-\frac{1}{8}\frac{p^4}{m^4c^4}\right)
=
\frac{p^2}{2m}-\frac{p^4}{8m^3c^2}.
\]
The first term is already \(H_0\), so the perturbation is:
\[
\boxed{H_{MV}=-\frac{\hat p^4}{8m^3c^2}.}
\]

---

## 2B) Spin-orbit coupling \(H_{SO}\) (with Thomas factor)
Physical idea (as in your clipboard): In the reference system of the electron, the nucleus in motion generates a magnetic field \(\vec B\) linked to the electric field \(\vec E=-\nabla V/e\). The interaction energy is:
\[
H_{int}=-\vec\mu_s\cdot\vec B,
\qquad
\vec\mu_s=-g\frac{e}{2m}\vec S \ (g\simeq2).
\]
By counting in a central potential and including the **precession of Thomas** (half the semi-classic result), you get:
\[
\boxed{H_{SO}= \frac{1}{2m^2c^2}\frac{1}{r}\frac{dV}{dr}\,\vec L\cdot \vec S.}
\]
For Coulomb:
\[
V(r)=-\frac{Ze^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{1}{r}
\Rightarrow
\frac{1}{r}\frac{dV}{dr}=\frac{Ze^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{1}{r^3},
\]
then
\[
\boxed{
H_{SO}=
\frac{Ze^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0\,m^2c^2}\frac{1}{r^3}\,\vec L\cdot\vec S.
}
\]

**Comment to be stated:** \(H_{SO}\) DO NOT store \(L_z\) and \(S_z\) separately, but store \(J^2\) and \(J_z\). Then you should work on \(|n\ell jm_j\rangle\) base.

---

## 2C) Darwin term \(H_D\)
In your notes (229-230) it is explained as "non-locality'/smearing on a Compton scale (zitterbewegung): the electron is not "sense' \(V(\vec r)\) punctually but an average on a small region \(\rho\sim \hbar/(mc)\).
\[
V(\vec r+\vec\rho)\approx V(\vec r)+\vec\rho\cdot\nabla V(\vec r)+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}\rho_i\rho_j\,\partial_i\partial_jV(\vec r)+\cdots
\]
By means of an isotropic distribution:
- \(\langle \vec\rho\rangle=0\) ⇒ linear term disappears
- \(\langle\rho_i\rho_j\rangle=\frac{\langle\rho^2\rangle}{3}\delta_{ij}\) ⇒ the square term becomes \(\propto \nabla^2V\)

The result (from Pauli/Foldy-Wouthuysen) is:
\[
\boxed{
H_D=\frac{\hbar^2}{8m^2c^2}\nabla^2V(r).
}
\]
For Coulomb:
\[
\nabla^2\left(\frac{1}{r}\right)=-4\pi\,\delta^{(3)}(\vec r)
\]
then
\[
\nabla^2V(r)= -\frac{Ze^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\nabla^2\left(\frac{1}{r}\right)
=
\frac{Ze^2}{\varepsilon_0}\,\delta^{(3)}(\vec r)
\]
and therefore:
\[
\boxed{
H_D=
\frac{\hbar^2}{8m^2c^2}\frac{Ze^2}{\varepsilon_0}\,\delta^{(3)}(\vec r).
}
\]
• Critical consequence: contributes **only** for states with \(\ell=0\) (the only with \(|\psi(0)|\neq0\)).

---

# 3) Perturbative strategy: first-order energy correction
Use the time-independent perturbations (DIM 7) on the first order:
\[
\boxed{
\Delta E^{(1)}=\langle n\ell jm_j|H'|n\ell jm_j\rangle
=
\langle H_{MV}\rangle+\langle H_{SO}\rangle+\langle H_D\rangle
}
\]
(The correction is independent of \(m_j\) because the problem is isotropic: no external field).

# 4) Calculation of the three contributions

## 4A) Mass-velocity contribution \(\langle H_{MV}\rangle\)
\[
\langle H_{MV}\rangle=-\frac{1}{8m^3c^2}\langle \hat p^4\rangle.
\]

Key Makeup (in your notes 230): from
\[
H_0=\frac{\hat p^2}{2m}+V
\]
and \(H_0\): \(H_0|\psi\rangle=E_n^{(0)}|\psi\rangle\),
Following
\[
\frac{\hat p^2}{2m}|\psi\rangle=(E_n^{(0)}-V)|\psi\rangle
\Rightarrow
\hat p^2|\psi\rangle=2m(E_n^{(0)}-V)|\psi\rangle.
\]
Then
\[
\langle \hat p^4\rangle
=
\langle \hat p^2\psi|\hat p^2\psi\rangle
=
(2m)^2\langle (E_n^{(0)}-V)^2\rangle.
\]
So:
\[
\langle \hat p^4\rangle=4m^2\left[(E_n^{(0)})^2-2E_n^{(0)}\langle V\rangle+\langle V^2\rangle\right].
\]

For Coulomb is worth the virial theorem:
\[
\boxed{\langle V\rangle=2E_n^{(0)}}
\]
and \(V^2=\left(\frac{Ze^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\right)^2\frac{1}{r^2}\), therefore it also serves:
\[
\boxed{
\left\langle \frac{1}{r^2}\right\rangle_{n\ell}
=\frac{Z^2}{a_0^2}\frac{1}{n^3\left(\ell+\tfrac12\right)}
}
\]
(with \(a_0=\frac{4\pi\varepsilon_0\hbar^2}{me^2}=\frac{\hbar}{mc\alpha}\)).

Replacing (standard result, as in `img/231.jpg`):
\[
\boxed{
\langle H_{MV}\rangle
=
mc^2(Z\alpha)^4\left[\frac{3}{8n^4}-\frac{1}{2n^3(\ell+\tfrac12)}\right].
}
\]

---

## 4B) Spin-orbit contribution \(\langle H_{SO}\rangle\)
\[
H_{SO}=\frac{1}{2m^2c^2}\frac{1}{r}\frac{dV}{dr}\,\vec L\cdot\vec S
\]
and the Coulomb:
\[
H_{SO}=\frac{Ze^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0m^2c^2}\frac{1}{r^3}\,\vec L\cdot\vec S.
\]

### (i) Angle part: \(\langle \vec L\cdot\vec S\rangle\)
Use identity:
\[
\vec J=\vec L+\vec S
\Rightarrow
J^2=L^2+S^2+2\vec L\cdot\vec S
\]
then
\[
\boxed{
\vec L\cdot\vec S=\frac{1}{2}\left(J^2-L^2-S^2\right).
}
\]
On eigenstates \(|n\ell jm_j\rangle\):
\[
J^2=\hbar^2 j(j+1),\quad L^2=\hbar^2\ell(\ell+1),\quad S^2=\hbar^2 s(s+1),\ s=\tfrac12\Rightarrow s(s+1)=\tfrac34
\]
then:
\[
\boxed{
\langle \vec L\cdot\vec S\rangle
=
\frac{\hbar^2}{2}\left[j(j+1)-\ell(\ell+1)-\frac34\right].
}
\]

### (ii) Radial part: \(\langle 1/r^3\rangle\)
For \(\ell\neq0\) (formula you have in `img/233.jpg`):
\[
\boxed{
\left\langle\frac{1}{r^3}\right\rangle_{n\ell}
=
\frac{Z^3}{a_0^3}\frac{1}{n^3\,\ell\left(\ell+\tfrac12\right)(\ell+1)}
\qquad (\ell\ge 1).
}
\]
(For \(\ell=0\) it would differ, but there \(L=0\Rightarrow H_{SO}=0\), so it doesn't need to.)

Multiplying everything (the "\(\hbar^2\) simplifies) you get:
\[
\boxed{
\langle H_{SO}\rangle
=
\frac{mc^2(Z\alpha)^4}{4n^3}\;
\frac{j(j+1)-\ell(\ell+1)-\tfrac34}{\ell\left(\ell+\tfrac12\right)(\ell+1)}
\qquad (\ell\ge1).
}
\]

---

## 4C) Contribution of Darwin \(\langle H_D\rangle\)
\[
H_D=\frac{\hbar^2}{8m^2c^2}\nabla^2V
=
\frac{\hbar^2}{8m^2c^2}\frac{Ze^2}{\varepsilon_0}\delta^{(3)}(\vec r).
\]
So:
\[
\langle H_D\rangle
=
\frac{\hbar^2}{8m^2c^2}\frac{Ze^2}{\varepsilon_0}\,|\psi_{n\ell m}(0)|^2.
\]
But \(|\psi(0)|\neq0\) **only for \(\ell=0\)**. For \(s\) states:
\[
|\psi_{n00}(0)|^2=\frac{1}{\pi}\left(\frac{Z}{a_0}\right)^3\frac{1}{n^3}.
\]
Compact result:
\[
\boxed{
\langle H_D\rangle
=
\frac{mc^2(Z\alpha)^4}{2n^3}\,\delta_{\ell 0}.
}
\]

# 5) Final sum and "miracle": depends only on \(j\)
Put it together:
\[
\Delta E_{fs}=\langle H_{MV}\rangle+\langle H_{SO}\rangle+\langle H_D\rangle.
\]

In your clipboard (`img/234`-`img/235`) you see the key point:

\boxed{\, i\hbar\,\dot c_n(t)=\lambda\sum_m V_{nm}(t)\,e^{i\omega_{nm}t}\,c_m(t)\,}
\]
with \(V_{nm}(t)=\langle n|\hat V(t)|m\rangle\) and \(\omega_{nm}=(E_n-E_m)/\hbar\).

2) **transition amplitude at first order** \(i\to f\):
\[
\boxed{\,c_f^{(1)}(t)= -\frac{i\lambda}{\hbar}\int_0^t V_{fi}(t')\,e^{i\omega_{fi}t'}\,dt'\,}
\]
and probability \(P_{i\to f}(t)=|c_f^{(1)}(t)|^2\) (order \(\lambda^2\)).

3) For **Constant perturbation** (access to \(t=0\)):
\[
P_{i\to f}(t)=\frac{4\lambda^2|V_{fi}|^2}{\hbar^2}\,\frac{\sin^2(\omega_{fi}t/2)}{\omega_{fi}^2}.
\]

4) For **periodical perturbation** ⇒ **resonance** when \(\omega\simeq\omega_{fi}\) (Bohr pulsation \(\omega_{fi}\)).

5) If the final states form a **continuum**, obtain the **Gold Rule of Fermi**:
\[
\boxed{\,W_{i\to f}=\frac{2\pi}{\hbar}\,|V_{fi}|^2\,\rho(E_f)\,}
\]
(with preserved energy in the sense of delta).

6) Connect \(W\), **lifetime** \(\tau\) and **width** \(\Gamma\):
\[
\boxed{\,\tau=\frac{1}{W}\,,\qquad \Gamma=\frac{\hbar}{\tau}=\hbar W\,}
\]

# 1) Setup: known system + perturbation
Suppose \(\hat H_0\) is soluble:
\[
\hat H_0|n\rangle = E_n|n\rangle,
\qquad
\langle n|m\rangle=\delta_{nm}.
\]

Then add a time dependent perturbation \(\hat V(t)\):
\[
\hat H(t)=\hat H_0+\lambda \hat V(t).
\]

Schrödinger's time dependent equation is:
\[
i\hbar \frac{\partial}{\partial t}|\psi(t)\rangle=\hat H(t)|\psi(t)\rangle.
\]

---

# 2) Change in constants: ansatz with unperturbed phases
If \(\lambda=0\), a \(|n\rangle\) eigenstates evolves as:
\[
|n,t\rangle = e^{-iE_n t/\hbar}|n\rangle.
\]

With perturbation, I look for the solution as a combination of these modes but with variable coefficients:
\[
\boxed{\,|\psi(t)\rangle=\sum_n c_n(t)\,e^{-iE_n t/\hbar}\,|n\rangle\,}
\]
This is exactly the **variation of the ** constants: if \(\hat V=0\), then \(c_n(t)\) are constant.

---

# 3) Derivation of the equation for the coefficients \(c_n(t)\)
Derivo \(|\psi(t)\rangle\):
\[
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}|\psi\rangle
=\sum_n \dot c_n(t)e^{-iE_n t/\hbar}|n\rangle
+\sum_n c_n(t)\left(-\frac{iE_n}{\hbar}\right)e^{-iE_n t/\hbar}|n\rangle.
\]

Multiply by \(i\hbar\):
\[
i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}|\psi\rangle
=
i\hbar\sum_n \dot c_n e^{-iE_n t/\hbar}|n\rangle
+\sum_n c_n E_n e^{-iE_n t/\hbar}|n\rangle.
\]

Now the right side:
\[
\hat H(t)|\psi\rangle=(\hat H_0+\lambda \hat V)\sum_m c_m e^{-iE_m t/\hbar}|m\rangle
=\sum_m c_m e^{-iE_m t/\hbar}\hat H_0|m\rangle+\lambda\sum_m c_m e^{-iE_m t/\hbar}\hat V|m\rangle.
\]

But \(\hat H_0|m\rangle=E_m|m\rangle\), then:
\[
\hat H(t)|\psi\rangle=\sum_m c_m E_m e^{-iE_m t/\hbar}|m\rangle + \lambda\sum_m c_m e^{-iE_m t/\hbar}\hat V|m\rangle.
\]

Comparison with the left side: the terms \(\sum c_n E_n e^{-iE_n t/\hbar}|n\rangle\) ** are deleted** (that's why the "ansatz" is intelligent).

\[
i\hbar\sum_n \dot c_n e^{-iE_n t/\hbar}|n\rangle
=
\lambda\sum_m c_m e^{-iE_m t/\hbar}\hat V|m\rangle.
\]

Project to \(\langle n|\):
\[
i\hbar\,\dot c_n(t)\,e^{-iE_n t/\hbar}
=
\lambda\sum_m c_m(t)\,e^{-iE_m t/\hbar}\,\langle n|\hat V(t)|m\rangle.
\]

Multiply by \(e^{+iE_n t/\hbar}\) and define:
\[
V_{nm}(t)\equiv \langle n|\hat V(t)|m\rangle,
\qquad
\omega_{nm}\equiv \frac{E_n-E_m}{\hbar}.
\]

I get:

\[
\boxed{\, i\hbar\,\dot c_n(t)=\lambda\sum_m V_{nm}(t)\,e^{i\omega_{nm}t}\,c_m(t)\,}
\]

This is the fundamental equation of the TDPT (interaction equation, identical to that of the interaction framework).

---

# 4) First order: transition amplitude \(i\to f\)
I take initial status:
\[
|\psi(0)\rangle=|i\rangle \quad\Rightarrow\quad c_i(0)=1,\; c_{n\neq i}(0)=0.
\]

At **first order** in \(\lambda\) nearest in the right member:
\[
c_i(t)\approx 1,\qquad c_{m\neq i}(t)\approx 0.
\]

Then for \(f\neq i\):
\[
i\hbar\,\dot c_f(t)=\lambda\,V_{fi}(t)\,e^{i\omega_{fi}t}.
\]

So:
\[
\dot c_f(t)= -\frac{i\lambda}{\hbar}V_{fi}(t)\,e^{i\omega_{fi}t}
\]

Integro 0 to \(t\):
\[
\boxed{\,c_f^{(1)}(t)= -\frac{i\lambda}{\hbar}\int_0^t V_{fi}(t')\,e^{i\omega_{fi}t'}\,dt'\,}
\]

The probability (in order \(\lambda^2\)) is:
\[
\boxed{\,P_{i\to f}(t)\simeq |c_f^{(1)}(t)|^2\,}
\]

---

# 5) Constant perturbation is switched on to \(t=0\)
Case: \(\hat V(t)=\hat V\) constant (or constant in the \(0<t<T\) stroke).

Then \(V_{fi}(t)=V_{fi}\) constant and:
\[
c_f^{(1)}(t)= -\frac{i\lambda}{\hbar}V_{fi}\int_0^t e^{i\omega_{fi}t'}dt'
= -\frac{i\lambda}{\hbar}V_{fi}\,\frac{e^{i\omega_{fi}t}-1}{i\omega_{fi}}.
\]

Simplify (\(-i/(i)=-1\)):
\[
c_f^{(1)}(t)= -\frac{\lambda V_{fi}}{\hbar}\,\frac{e^{i\omega_{fi}t}-1}{\omega_{fi}}.
\]

Usage \(e^{i\omega t}-1=2ie^{i\omega t/2}\sin(\omega t/2)\):
\[
c_f^{(1)}(t)= -\frac{2i\lambda V_{fi}}{\hbar}\,e^{i\omega_{fi}t/2}\,\frac{\sin(\omega_{fi}t/2)}{\omega_{fi}}.
\]

Probability:
\[
\boxed{\,P_{i\to f}(t)=\frac{4\lambda^2|V_{fi}|^2}{\hbar^2}\,\frac{\sin^2(\omega_{fi}t/2)}{\omega_{fi}^2}\,}
\]

### Essential comment
This is a "bell" type \(\text{sinc}^2\) centered on \(\omega_{fi}=0\) (i.e. \(E_f\simeq E_i\)).  
For long time it narrows: the more time you observe, the more selective it is in energy.

---

# 6) Periodic perturbation: Bohr resonance and pulse
I take a typical case (as in the clipboard): harmonic perturbation
\[
\hat V(t)=\hat W\cos(\omega t)=\frac{\hat W}{2}\left(e^{i\omega t}+e^{-i\omega t}\right).
\]
So:
\[
V_{fi}(t)=\frac{W_{fi}}{2}\left(e^{i\omega t}+e^{-i\omega t}\right),
\qquad W_{fi}=\langle f|\hat W|i\rangle.
\]

I insert into the width:
\[
c_f^{(1)}(t)= -\frac{i\lambda}{\hbar}\int_0^t \frac{W_{fi}}{2}(e^{i\omega t'}+e^{-i\omega t'})e^{i\omega_{fi}t'}dt'.
\]

I get two integrals:
\[
c_f^{(1)}(t)= -\frac{i\lambda W_{fi}}{2\hbar}\left[
\int_0^t e^{i(\omega_{fi}+\omega)t'}dt'
+
\int_0^t e^{i(\omega_{fi}-\omega)t'}dt'
\right].
\]

So:
\[
c_f^{(1)}(t)= -\frac{i\lambda W_{fi}}{2\hbar}\left[
\frac{e^{i(\omega_{fi}+\omega)t}-1}{i(\omega_{fi}+\omega)}
+
\frac{e^{i(\omega_{fi}-\omega)t}-1}{i(\omega_{fi}-\omega)}
\right].
\]

The physical content is here:

## Resonance
The second term shall explode when:
\[
\boxed{\omega \simeq \omega_{fi}}
\]
where
\[
\boxed{\omega_{fi}=\frac{E_f-E_i}{\hbar}}
\]
is the **pulsation of Bohr**.

- \(\omega \approx \omega_{fi}\): **absorbing** (the field provides energy \(\hbar\omega\)).
- \(\omega \approx -\omega_{fi}\): **stimulated emission** (in practice \(E_f<E_i\) and the energy goes to the field).

In energy terms:
\[
E_f \approx E_i \pm \hbar\omega.
\]

---

# 7) From the discrete spectrum to the continuum: derivation of Fermi's golden rule
If the final states \(|f\rangle\) form a **continuum** (or a near-continuous dense), I don't look at the single probability towards a single \(f\), but the **total transition probability** adding up to so many final states.

The key thing (within long time limits) is that an energy delta appears. Formally:
\[
\frac{\sin^2(\Delta\omega\,t/2)}{\Delta\omega^2}
\ \xrightarrow[t\ \text{grande}]{}\ \frac{\pi t}{2}\,\delta(\Delta\omega)
\]
where \(\Delta\omega\) is the resonance discharge (e.g. \(\omega_{fi}-\omega\)).

Switching from \(\delta(\Delta\omega)\) to \(\delta(\Delta E)\):
\[
\Delta\omega=\frac{\Delta E}{\hbar}\quad\Rightarrow\quad \delta(\Delta\omega)=\hbar\,\delta(\Delta E).
\]

## Final form (Golden Rule)
For a transition to a continuous with state density \(\rho(E_f)\), you get a **rate** (probability per unit of time):

\[
\boxed{\,W_{i\to f}=\frac{2\pi}{\hbar}\,|V_{fi}|^2\,\rho(E_f)\,}
\]

- If the perturbation is periodic (frequency \(\omega\)), the energy is:
  \[
  E_f = E_i \pm \hbar\omega
  \]
  then \(\rho(E_f)\) should be evaluated in \(E_i\pm\hbar\omega\).

**Physical translation:** in a continuous, the probability doesn't sway and just squeezes, but ** grows linearly over time**:
\[
P(t)\approx W\,t
\]
(until \(P\ll 1\), i.e. in the troublesome regime).

---

# 8) Lifetime \(\tau\) and linewidth \(\Gamma\)
If an initial level \(|i\rangle\) can decay in many continuous states, the Golden Rule provides the total rate:
\[
W=\sum_f W_{i\to f}
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
\boxed{\tau=\frac{1}{W}}.
\]

The **energy width** associated (natural linewidth) is:
\[
\boxed{\Gamma=\frac{\hbar}{\tau}=\hbar W.}
\]

### Why is it physical?
- If the state lives little (\(\tau\) small), its energy is little defined: the spectral line widens (\(\Gamma\) large).
- Conceptual connection with energy-time uncertainty: \(\Delta E \sim \hbar/\tau\).

---

# Symbol Dictionary (to know one by one)
- \(\hat H(t)\): Total Hamiltonian time dependent.
- \(\hat H_0\): Hamiltonian not disturbed (stationary, resolved).
- \(\hat V(t)\): perturbation (time dependent).
- \(\lambda\): small parameter (order count); often in the end \(\lambda=1\).
- \(|n\rangle\): \(\hat H_0\) eigenstates.
- \(E_n\): eigenvalues of \(\hat H_0\) (unperturbed energy).
- \(|\psi(t)\rangle\): System status.
- \(c_n(t)\): Slow coefficients in the base of \(\hat H_0\).
- \(V_{nm}(t)=\langle n|\hat V(t)|m\rangle\): perturbation matrix elements.
- \(\omega_{nm}=(E_n-E_m)/\hbar\): Pulsation associated with jump \(m\to n\).
- \(\omega_{fi}\): **Bohr pulsation** (between \(i\) and \(f\) levels).
- \(\hat W\): operator multiplying the harmonic part, if \(\hat V(t)=\hat W\cos\omega t\).
- \(W_{fi}=\langle f|\hat W|i\rangle\): matrix element for harmonic perturbation.
- \(P_{i\to f}(t)\): Transition probability \(i\to f\) at time \(t\).
- \(\rho(E)\): continuous state density (states per energy unit).
- \(W\): transition rate (probability per unit of time).
- \(\tau\): average life (\(\tau=1/W\)).
- \(\Gamma\): energy width (\(\Gamma=\hbar/\tau\)).
- \(\delta(\cdot)\): Dirac delta (energy selector) “in resonance”).

---

# Physical meaning (to be said well, without getting lost in the calculation)
- The time-dependent perturbations **induces transitions** between \(\hat H_0\) eigenstates.
- The phases \(e^{i\omega_{fi}t}\) mean that the integral is added only if you are close to resonance: the **pulsation of Bohr** is born.
- For a continuous of final states, the probability does not remain oscillating: mediating/suming on so many endings becomes a **constant rate** → Golden Rule.
- \(\tau\) and \(\Gamma\) are experimental translations: "How much lives a layer" and "how wide the spectral line" is.
